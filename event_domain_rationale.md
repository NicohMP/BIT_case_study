| 1 | Monetary policy surprises (FOMC)       | Event‑study work on FOMC surprises shows statistically significant negative stock returns when policy tightens unexpectedly, with growth and tech sectors generally more sensitive than defensives. This justifies a high prior that AI mega‑caps, priced on long‑dated cash flows, sell off disproportionately on surprise hikes. Key refs: Sekandary & Bask on monetary policy surprises and US stock returns [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0148619522000625](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0148619522000625), and Gormsen & Lazarus on interest rates and equity valuations [https://www.nber.org/papers/w34814](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34814). | Monetary policy surprise studies document broad equity sensitivity, but manufacturing and cyclicals like semis have more mixed reactions because earnings cyclicality and global trade matter alongside discount‑rate effects. The same event‑study evidence supports a meaningful yet slightly lower prior than for pure growth platforms. See Sekandary & Bask (FOMC surprises and stock returns) [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0148619522000625](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0148619522000625) and the interest‑rate / valuation decomposition in Gormsen & Lazarus [https://www.nber.org/papers/w34814](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34814). | High‑duration SaaS / cloud names behave like textbook “long‑duration equities”: event‑driven sell‑offs around hawkish surprises have repeatedly hit this segment hardest in practice. The equity‑duration literature supports stronger sensitivity of high‑growth, low‑cash‑payout firms to discount‑rate shocks, matching the profile of cloud and dev‑infra names. See Golez & Koudijs on equity duration [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304405X25001229](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304405X25001229) and the global duration/valuation analysis in Gormsen & Lazarus [https://www.nber.org/papers/w34814](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34814). | Crypto infra equities are not classic DCF assets, but empirical work and market commentary show that FOMC surprises still transmit via global risk appetite and liquidity, affecting exchange volumes and miner financing conditions. The mechanism is thus indirect (through the macro risk channel rather than discount factors on cash flows) but strong enough to warrant a medium prior. See general evidence on monetary policy surprises and stock returns [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0148619522000625](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0148619522000625) and rate/valuation decompositions [https://www.nber.org/papers/w34814](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34814). | Retail brokers and consumer finance platforms have a dual exposure: surprise hikes change both equity discount rates and the credit/liquidity environment that drives trading volumes and risk demand. FOMC event‑study evidence plus the duration literature justify a high prior here, similar to growth tech but with an added channel via household and margin borrowing conditions. For the discount‑rate/valuation channel see Gormsen & Lazarus [https://www.nber.org/papers/w34814](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34814), and for stock‑return reactions to policy surprises see Sekandary & Bask [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0148619522000625](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0148619522000625). | Digital health and insurtech are typically mid‑cap growth names whose valuations respond to monetary‑policy‑driven discount‑rate shocks, but sector‑specific regulation and fundamentals add noise. Event‑study work on policy surprises and stock returns supports a clear negative relation, yet without the extreme duration multiples seen in hyper‑growth SaaS. See the monetary policy surprise evidence [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0148619522000625](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0148619522000625) and general equity‑duration findings in Golez & Koudijs [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304405X25001229](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304405X25001229). |
| 2 | Real yields / long rates               | The equity‑duration literature and recent NBER work show that movements in real discount rates (net of growth/risk components) map strongly into valuations of long‑duration equities, with tech/growth featuring higher exposure. AI platforms fit this profile: large parts of their value come from long‑horizon projects and terminal value. See Gormsen & Lazarus (decomposing real‑rate drivers and equity valuations) [https://www.nber.org/papers/w34814](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34814) and Golez & Koudijs on duration and predictability [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304405X25001229](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304405X25001229). | For semiconductors, real‑rate shocks matter mainly through valuation and capex cycles, but earnings are also strongly driven by inventory, pricing, and geopolitical factors. The literature finds only modest unconditional duration for many sectors, consistent with a medium prior rather than a dominant discount‑rate story. See Golez & Koudijs on time‑varying equity duration [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304405X25001229](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304405X25001229) and the cross‑country real‑rate/valuation decomposition in Gormsen & Lazarus [https://www.nber.org/papers/w34814](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34814). | Cloud / enterprise software is almost the definition of a long‑duration cash‑flow profile, and empirical factor work shows strong sensitivity of high‑growth software valuations to long‑rate and real‑rate regimes. The duration papers thus support a high prior that real‑yield spikes compress multiples here more than in short‑duration sectors. Again see Gormsen & Lazarus [https://www.nber.org/papers/w34814](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34814) and Golez & Koudijs [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304405X25001229](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304405X25001229). | For crypto infra, the mechanism from real yields to equity is via the macro “store‑of‑value vs real‑yield” tradeoff and risk sentiment that drive crypto‑asset prices, volumes, and funding conditions. While there is less formal, peer‑reviewed event‑study work here, the same discount‑rate logic plus observed co‑movement of crypto with real‑rate regimes justify a medium prior. The structural mapping from real rates to equity valuations in Gormsen & Lazarus provides the template [https://www.nber.org/papers/w34814](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34814). | Fintech lenders and brokers experience a nuanced effect: higher real rates can expand net interest margins but compress valuations and reduce risk‑taking by households. Macro/asset‑pricing work on real‑rate shocks and cross‑sectional returns suggests meaningful, time‑varying exposures rather than a one‑way effect, consistent with a medium prior. See the general interest‑rate / equity‑valuation decomposition in Gormsen & Lazarus [https://www.nber.org/papers/w34814](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34814) and equity‑duration evidence in Golez & Koudijs [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304405X25001229](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304405X25001229). | Digital health / insurtech valuations are rate‑sensitive growth assets, but the sector also reacts to healthcare policy and insurance‑specific drivers that can dominate rate effects in certain windows. The equity‑duration literature justifies a non‑trivial but not extreme prior: they look more like generic growth than the purest high‑multiple SaaS cohort. See Golez & Koudijs [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304405X25001229](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304405X25001229) and Gormsen & Lazarus [https://www.nber.org/papers/w34814](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34814). |
| 3 | US–China semiconductor export controls | Export controls on advanced chips directly constrain AI hardware supply and China‑related demand, which in turn affects AI capex plans and the cost of training/inference at hyperscalers. ITIF’s modeling shows that severe decoupling could cut tens of billions in US chip sales and R&D, implying tighter and more expensive AI compute for platforms. See ITIF “Decoupling Risks: How Semiconductor Export Controls Could Harm US Chipmakers and Innovation” [https://itif.org/publications/2025/11/10/decoupling-risks-semiconductor-export-controls-harm-us-chipmakers-innovation/](https://itif.org/publications/2025/11/10/decoupling-risks-semiconductor-export-controls-harm-us-chipmakers-innovation/). | Semiconductors and compute infrastructure are the **direct target** of US–China export controls, with ITIF’s report quantifying initial revenue losses of ~$77bn and large R&D and employment impacts under full decoupling. That makes this a first‑order, repeatedly documented mechanism for this domain. See ITIF’s detailed model and scenario analysis [https://itif.org/publications/2025/11/10/decoupling-risks-semiconductor-export-controls-harm-us-chipmakers-innovation/](https://itif.org/publications/2025/11/10/decoupling-risks-semiconductor-export-controls-harm-us-chipmakers-innovation/) and their broader policy analysis [https://itif.org/publications/2025/12/19/export-controls-should-advance-us-semiconductor-leadership/](https://itif.org/publications/2025/12/19/export-controls-should-advance-us-semiconductor-leadership/). | Cloud / dev‑infra firms consume the constrained hardware and often sell into China, so export controls work through higher unit costs, constrained capacity, and reduced growth in restricted markets. ITIF emphasizes that export controls in semis propagate into ICT sectors like AI and cloud via reduced chip availability and higher prices. This supports a medium prior for cloud: see their modeling of downstream ICT impacts [https://itif.org/publications/2025/11/10/decoupling-risks-semiconductor-export-controls-harm-us-chipmakers-innovation/](https://itif.org/publications/2025/11/10/decoupling-risks-semiconductor-export-controls-harm-us-chipmakers-innovation/). | Crypto miners and related infra rely heavily on GPUs/ASICs whose supply and pricing can be affected by export controls to China, especially in high‑end AI GPUs that are also repurposed for compute‑intensive crypto workloads. While ITIF’s work focuses on broader ICT rather than crypto specifically, the same channel—constrained advanced chips and shifting supply to non‑US vendors—implies meaningful second‑order effects. See ITIF’s quantification of export‑control impacts on chip markets and downstream ICT [https://itif.org/publications/2025/11/10/decoupling-risks-semiconductor-export-controls-harm-us-chipmakers-innovation/](https://itif.org/publications/2025/11/10/decoupling-risks-semiconductor-export-controls-harm-us-chipmakers-innovation/). | For fintech / consumer financial platforms, there is no clean, recurring mechanism by which chip export rules translate into their P&L beyond generic macro spillovers (growth, employment, risk‑off episodes). ITIF highlights impacts on chipmakers and ICT but not on financial intermediation or brokerage economics, supporting a near‑zero structural prior for this domain. See again ITIF’s export‑control assessment [https://itif.org/publications/2025/11/10/decoupling-risks-semiconductor-export-controls-harm-us-chipmakers-innovation/](https://itif.org/publications/2025/11/10/decoupling-risks-semiconductor-export-controls-harm-us-chipmakers-innovation/). | Digital health / insurtech is even further removed: any effect would be via higher cloud/compute pricing or macro conditions, which you already capture with other signals (rates, power, IT cycle). Since policy and reimbursement drive this space far more than chip exports, the ITIF evidence supports a baseline of negligible direct influence. See ITIF’s focus on semis and ICT, with no specific channel to healthcare/insurance [https://itif.org/publications/2025/11/10/decoupling-risks-semiconductor-export-controls-harm-us-chipmakers-innovation/](https://itif.org/publications/2025/11/10/decoupling-risks-semiconductor-export-controls-harm-us-chipmakers-innovation/). |
| 4 | Taiwan geopolitical risk               | Research on Taiwan’s semiconductor industry highlights its centrality to advanced chip fabrication and the vulnerability of global supply chains to quarantine, blockade, or conflict scenarios in the Taiwan Strait. For AI platforms, this translates into tail risks to GPU availability, data‑center build‑out, and regional operations. See Liu et al. “From vulnerabilities to resilience: Taiwan’s semiconductor industry and geopolitical challenges” [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308596125000485](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308596125000485) and related analysis of geopolitical risk to Taiwan’s chip sector (e.g. APSA preprint) [https://preprints.apsanet.org/engage/api-gateway/apsa/assets/orp/resource/item/64df036a01042bc1cc413c68/original/a-discussion-on-geopolitical-risk-of-taiwan-semiconductor-industry.pdf](https://preprints.apsanet.org/engage/api-gateway/apsa/assets/orp/resource/item/64df036a01042bc1cc413c68/original/a-discussion-on-geopolitical-risk-of-taiwan-semiconductor-industry.pdf). | Taiwan‑focused studies emphasize that a large share of advanced fabrication sits on the island, and that quarantine/blockade scenarios could significantly disrupt global semiconductor supply. That makes Taiwan geopolitical risk a first‑order, persistent threat to semis and compute infrastructure. See Liu et al. on Taiwan’s semiconductor supply‑chain vulnerabilities [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308596125000485](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308596125000485) and the APSA discussion paper on geopolitical risk for Taiwan’s semiconductor industry [https://preprints.apsanet.org/engage/api-gateway/apsa/assets/orp/resource/item/64df036a01042bc1cc413c68/original/a-discussion-on-geopolitical-risk-of-taiwan-semiconductor-industry.pdf](https://preprints.apsanet.org/engage/api-gateway/apsa/assets/orp/resource/item/64df036a01042bc1cc413c68/original/a-discussion-on-geopolitical-risk-of-taiwan-semiconductor-industry.pdf). | Cloud / dev‑infra depends on reliable supply of server CPUs, GPUs, and networking hardware, so the Taiwan risk literature implies medium‑to‑high exposure through potential hardware shortages and cost spikes. Liu et al. explicitly frame Taiwan’s fab ecosystem as a critical node whose disruption would cascade into ICT, including AI and cloud. This justifies a medium prior: see their scenario analysis of quarantine/blockade [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308596125000485](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308596125000485). | Crypto infra is linked more weakly: mining hardware and some infra equipment would face supply disruptions, but miners can shift to non‑Taiwan fabs over time, and the main risk comes via global macro stress. The Taiwan semiconductor risk papers show the centrality of Taiwan for advanced nodes, yet crypto ASIC production is more geographically diversified than AI leading‑edge nodes. See Liu et al. for supply‑chain concentration and scenarios [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308596125000485](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308596125000485). | Fintech / CFP would mainly feel Taiwan shocks via broad market and economic risk‑off, not through a sector‑specific supply chain. The Taiwan geopolitics literature around semis focuses on ICT and manufacturing rather than financial services, backing a low but non‑zero prior as global volatility and funding conditions change. Again, see Liu et al. for the central supply‑chain role without a direct financial‑services channel [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308596125000485](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308596125000485). | Digital health / insurtech are largely downstream service and insurance businesses; Taiwan‑specific chip supply shocks would influence them only via macro conditions and general tech stack costs. The semis‑focused geopolitical risk work provides no evidence of a direct, recurring linkage to healthcare or insurance operating models, supporting a low prior. See Liu et al. for the focus on chip supply chains rather than service‑sector impacts [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308596125000485](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308596125000485). |
| 5 | Crypto regulation regime changes       | MiCA (EU) and similar regimes define the licensing, disclosure, and conduct rules for crypto‑asset service providers and token issuers, with direct implications for Big Tech only when they enter crypto/payments. ESMA’s MiCA materials show that core obligations target crypto‑asset white papers, stablecoins, and CASPs—not generic AI/cloud platforms—so the prior is low, limited to optional web3/crypto initiatives. See ESMA’s MiCA overview [https://www.esma.europa.eu/esmas-activities/digital-finance-and-innovation/markets-crypto-assets-regulation-mica](https://www.esma.europa.eu/esmas-activities/digital-finance-and-innovation/markets-crypto-assets-regulation-mica) and AMF’s MiCA explainer PDF [https://www.amf-france.org/sites/institutionnel/files/pdf/70237/en/Crypto-asset_markets_the_MiCA_regulation_adopted_by_the_European_Parliament.pdf](https://www.amf-france.org/sites/institutionnel/files/pdf/70237/en/Crypto-asset_markets_the_MiCA_regulation_adopted_by_the_European_Parliament.pdf). | Semiconductor firms are not within the primary perimeter of crypto‑asset regulation, which focuses on tokens, stablecoins, and service providers like exchanges and custodians. ESMA and national regulators treat crypto as a financial‑market issue rather than a hardware or manufacturing issue, so there is no structural mechanism for repeated direct impact here. See ESMA’s MiCA framework [https://www.esma.europa.eu/esmas-activities/digital-finance-and-innovation/markets-crypto-assets-regulation-mica](https://www.esma.europa.eu/esmas-activities/digital-finance-and-innovation/markets-crypto-assets-regulation-mica). | Cloud / dev‑infra can host CASPs and blockchain nodes, but MiCA and similar rules regulate the CASPs themselves, not generic cloud providers. Regulatory documents emphasize disclosure, capital, and conduct requirements at the financial‑service layer rather than underlying infrastructure, justifying only a low prior for cloud. See ESMA’s MiCA documentation [https://www.esma.europa.eu/esmas-activities/digital-finance-and-innovation/markets-crypto-assets-regulation-mica](https://www.esma.europa.eu/esmas-activities/digital-finance-and-innovation/markets-crypto-assets-regulation-mica) and AMF’s summary [https://www.amf-france.org/sites/institutionnel/files/pdf/70237/en/Crypto-asset_markets_the_MiCA_regulation_adopted_by_the_European_Parliament.pdf](https://www.amf-france.org/sites/institutionnel/files/pdf/70237/en/Crypto-asset_markets_the_MiCA_regulation_adopted_by_the_European_Parliament.pdf). | Crypto regulation is **designed** for this domain: MiCA and SEC/ESMA guidance govern token issuance, custody, trading, stablecoins, and CASPs, directly affecting product scope, leverage, and compliance cost. Event‑style market moves around major regulatory announcements and detailed regime design (e.g. MiCA’s implementation timelines) support a high prior. See ESMA’s MiCA page [https://www.esma.europa.eu/esmas-activities/digital-finance-and-innovation/markets-crypto-assets-regulation-mica](https://www.esma.europa.eu/esmas-activities/digital-finance-and-innovation/markets-crypto-assets-regulation-mica) and AMF’s MiCA note [https://www.amf-france.org/sites/institutionnel/files/pdf/70237/en/Crypto-asset_markets_the_MiCA_regulation_adopted_by_the_European_Parliament.pdf](https://www.amf-france.org/sites/institutionnel/files/pdf/70237/en/Crypto-asset_markets_the_MiCA_regulation_adopted_by_the_European_Parliament.pdf). | Fintech platforms that intermediate crypto (retail brokers offering tokens, neobanks with crypto rails) fall partly under MiCA/CASP rules and similar regimes elsewhere, which change their compliance cost and product set. However, these firms also have large non‑crypto business lines, so the regime is a material but not existential driver, fitting a medium prior. See MiCA’s scope for crypto‑asset service providers [https://www.esma.europa.eu/esmas-activities/digital-finance-and-innovation/markets-crypto-assets-regulation-mica](https://www.esma.europa.eu/esmas-activities/digital-finance-and-innovation/markets-crypto-assets-regulation-mica) and the AMF’s description of affected services [https://www.amf-france.org/sites/institutionnel/files/pdf/70237/en/Crypto-asset_markets_the_MiCA_regulation_adopted_by_the_European_Parliament.pdf](https://www.amf-france.org/sites/institutionnel/files/pdf/70237/en/Crypto-asset_markets_the_MiCA_regulation_adopted_by_the_European_Parliament.pdf). | Digital health / insurtech are outside the perimeter of crypto‑asset market rules in baseline, except for highly idiosyncratic projects. Regulatory texts from ESMA and national authorities frame MiCA as a regime for crypto‑asset markets and intermediaries rather than healthcare or insurance, supporting a zero prior. See MiCA’s sectoral focus [https://www.esma.europa.eu/esmas-activities/digital-finance-and-innovation/markets-crypto-assets-regulation-mica](https://www.esma.europa.eu/esmas-activities/digital-finance-and-innovation/markets-crypto-assets-regulation-mica). |
| 6 | AI regulation + Big Tech enforcement   | Competition and antitrust studies show that enforcement actions (raids, infringement decisions, remedies) generate large, negative abnormal returns for targeted firms, reflecting expected losses in profitability and strategic freedom. Combined with AI‑specific regulation (e.g. EU AI Act–style obligations on high‑risk systems), this underpins a high prior for AI & Big Tech as direct targets whose business models and compliance costs are materially affected. See Günster & van Dijk on stock‑market reactions to European antitrust decisions [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0144818815000721](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0144818815000721) and EU policy material on AI and platform regulation (e.g. EC AI policy overview) [https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/european-approach-artificial-intelligence](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/european-approach-artificial-intelligence). | AI‑specific rules focus more on high‑risk applications and providers of AI systems than on pure hardware suppliers, so semis & compute are affected mainly via induced changes in AI demand and deployment. However, European and national policy documents explicitly link AI regulation to semiconductor and compute infrastructure needs (e.g. EU Chips Act and AI policy being developed in tandem), giving a meaningful but indirect channel: stricter rules can slow or reshape AI workloads, which feeds back into AI‑chip demand. See the EU’s AI policy overview [https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/european-approach-artificial-intelligence](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/european-approach-artificial-intelligence) and the EU Chips Act summary [https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32023R1781](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32023R1781). | Cloud / dev‑infra is central to AI deployment: AI regulation directly touches providers of AI services and often assumes implementation via cloud platforms operated by a small set of hyperscalers. Policy texts and impact assessments for the EU AI framework describe obligations on providers and deployers of AI systems (documentation, risk management, monitoring), which map straight into the offerings and compliance stack of cloud‑ and ML‑platform providers. See the European Commission’s AI Act page and policy docs [https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/european-approach-artificial-intelligence](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/european-approach-artificial-intelligence) and an overview of obligations for AI service providers from the EC’s Q&A [https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/QANDA_21_1683](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/QANDA_21_1683). | Crypto infra is largely outside the scope of AI‑specific regulation and Big Tech antitrust, except where exchanges or platforms use AI for surveillance, risk, or customer interfaces and thus incur generic high‑risk‑AI obligations. Regulatory texts and antitrust cases to date focus on platform gatekeeping, advertising, search, and consumer AI applications rather than on crypto exchanges or miners, supporting a zero structural prior. For scope, see the Commission’s AI‑Act overview [https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/european-approach-artificial-intelligence](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/european-approach-artificial-intelligence) and the antitrust event‑study evidence that centers on classic industrial and services firms [https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0144818815000721](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0144818815000721). | Fintech / CFP uses AI for underwriting, fraud, and customer service, so AI rules can increase compliance overhead and constrain some models, but sector‑specific financial regulation remains the main risk driver. EU AI policy material explicitly lists credit‑scoring and some financial AI uses as high‑risk applications subject to stricter requirements, implying a modest but real channel via model governance and costs. See the EC AI‑Act Q&A that lists credit scoring as high‑risk [https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/QANDA_21_1683](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/QANDA_21_1683) and the overall European AI regulatory approach [https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/european-approach-artificial-intelligence](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/european-approach-artificial-intelligence). | Digital health & insurtech are more directly hit by AI rules than generic software because medical diagnostics, triage, and health‑risk scoring fall squarely into high‑risk AI categories. EU AI policy explicitly treats medical devices and health‑related decision systems as high‑risk, requiring stricter conformity assessment, documentation, and human oversight, which raises compliance cost and liability exposure for AI‑enabled digital‑health players. See the EC AI‑Act material where health‑related AI systems are listed among high‑risk uses [https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/QANDA_21_1683](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/QANDA_21_1683) and the broader European AI policy overview [https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/european-approach-artificial-intelligence](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/european-approach-artificial-intelligence). |
| 7 | Data-center power / grid constraints   | McKinsey and other industry analyses document that AI data‑center growth is increasingly bottlenecked by access to power and grid capacity, with hyperscalers facing permitting delays and regional power shortages that cap how much compute they can deploy. This directly constrains the rollout of AI training and inference clusters for Big Tech platforms and raises unit costs, justifying a high prior.
